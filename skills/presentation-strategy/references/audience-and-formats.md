# Audience, purpose, and delivery format

Use these patterns to choose a structure, not as mandatory slide lists. Respect supplied content, order, audience, and length. A presentation may combine purposes, but its main audience outcome should remain clear.

## Audience analysis that changes the result

Identify what people already know, what they care about, what they can decide, and what might prevent agreement or understanding. Consider language, cultural context, access needs, and viewing conditions. Avoid stereotypes based on job title, age, nationality, or seniority.

For mixed audiences, make the main explanation usable by the people responsible for the outcome while preserving specialist evidence in an appendix, notes, linked source, or companion as appropriate. If a critical technical distinction determines the decision, explain it in the main story.

## Presentation patterns

| Type and audience | Audience question | Useful content and order | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision or strategy for executives, boards, or sponsors | What should we decide and what follows? | Decision needed, recommendation if supported, alternatives, evidence, costs, risks, dependencies, and implications of delay | Background consumes the meeting and the actual decision stays implicit |
| Proposal or sales presentation for clients | Does this address our problem and can you deliver? | Client situation, desired outcomes, proposed approach, credible proof, scope, exclusions, commercials when requested, next step | Seller biography and generic capabilities crowd out the client's problem |
| Investor presentation | Why this opportunity, team, and investment? | Product and problem, evidence of demand or traction, market with assumptions, business model, differentiation, team, financial context, funding ask | Unsupported market size or traction presented without dates and definitions |
| Project update for sponsors and delivery stakeholders | What changed and where is intervention needed? | Outcomes since the last update, baseline versus current position, deviations, dependencies, risks, decisions, accountable next steps | Activity lists replace progress, or status colors conceal missing evidence |
| Product demo for users, buyers, or internal teams | How does this help complete a real task? | User situation, task, actual workflow, observable result, limitations, relevant next step | Feature tour has no user goal, or a mockup is presented as working behavior |
| Technical or research talk for specialists | How does it work and what supports the conclusion? | Question, essential context, mechanism or method, results, comparison, tradeoffs, limitations | Abstract benefits replace mechanisms, or details arrive before the audience can interpret them |
| Training or workshop for learners | What should I understand or be able to do? | Learning outcome, prior knowledge, explanation, worked example, practice or discussion, feedback, recap | A content dump has no opportunity to apply or check understanding |
| Keynote or awareness talk for a broad audience | Why does this matter and what should I remember? | Central idea, relatable situation, selected evidence, concrete examples, implications or invitation | Inspirational phrases and imagery replace a defensible idea |

An investor pitch is a distinct persuasive setting. Do not copy its structure into a project update or a training session. [Y Combinator's Demo Day guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/) provides practitioner advice for a specific fundraising context, not a universal deck formula.

## Delivery modes

### Live, in person or remote

Slides share attention with the speaker. Keep essential evidence readable at the expected viewing size and use notes for context that the speaker will supply. Account for questions and transitions when estimating time. Do not infer a universal slides-per-minute rate or enforce a fixed word count regardless of content.

For remote or hybrid settings, verify that screenshots, labels, and charts remain legible in a shared window. Do not make a laser pointer, subtle color change, or animation the only way to understand the point.

For demos, plan a fallback appropriate to the task, such as screenshots or a recording of the relevant flow. Keep its provenance clear; a recording or mockup does not prove current live behavior. Test essential media in the intended application when possible.

### Independently read

Supply enough context for someone who did not hear the speaker. Use a skimmable hierarchy, explicit relationships, legible data, and accessible sources. A page may contain more information than a live slide, but visual structure must still help the reader. Keep material assumptions and disclosures in the exported content.

[Duarte's Slidedocs guidance](https://www.duarte.com/resources/guides-tools/slidedocs-visual-documents/) distinguishes visual documents for independent reading from slides watched during a talk. This is a useful format distinction, not proof that every meeting needs a second deck.

### Combined use

Choose within the requested scope: a presentation plus a short handout, a reading version, notes pages, or a concise main deck with a usable appendix. Explain any consequential tradeoff rather than silently doubling the deliverables. Speaker notes help only if the audience can access them in the delivered format.

## Small planning artifacts

Use a brief paragraph for a clear task. A substantial deck may benefit from a compact storyboard:

| Slide or section | Audience question / purpose | Point and evidence | Planned visual | Speaker context |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appropriate title | What this contributes to the sequence | Supported claim, source, or acknowledged gap | Visual that explains the content | Context omitted from a live slide |

This is a format example, not a requirement to fill every column. Preserve the requested slide count and omit a cover when it would consume a one-slide request.
