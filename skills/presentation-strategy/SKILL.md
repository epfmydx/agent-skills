# Presentation Strategy & Storytelling

Help the audience understand, decide, or act. Establish the message and evidence before polishing slides. This skill supplies communication judgment and can operate independently or alongside a development team. It does not prescribe models or launch workers.

## Match the requested work

- Preserve the task's mode: research, critique, outline, rewrite, storyboard, or deck creation. Advice does not authorize editing a deck. A copy edit does not authorize restructuring the argument or replacing the design.
- Follow the user's audience, brand, template, source material, language, slide count, and output format. A reference supplied for content alone does not establish a design to copy.
- Scale the work. A single-slide correction needs its relevant checks, not a full discovery process, research pass, or rehearsal package. Do not add slides, notes, extra versions, or deliverables merely to complete this workflow.
- For deck creation or editing, use an available presentation-authoring skill and its tool, format, rendering, and delivery instructions. Resolve it from the current environment; do not assume a specific vendor, plugin, path, or paid service. If authoring is unavailable, complete the usable outline or storyboard and state that no deck file was produced.
- Use an available writing-style skill when the user requests their voice or when writing on their behalf warrants it. Preserve supplied terminology, quotations, and technical distinctions. Do not infer a whole organization's style from a few informal messages.

## Establish the presentation brief

Use supplied context first. Ask only for missing information that materially changes the result; keep progressing on independent work. State reasonable assumptions when useful rather than inventing stakeholder decisions.

Identify the relevant inputs:

| Input | Decision it informs |
| --- | --- |
| Audience and their role | Existing knowledge, priorities, objections, authority, and accessibility needs |
| Purpose and desired response | What people should understand, decide, learn, or do afterwards |
| Delivery mode and setting | Live, remote, recorded, independently read, or combined use |
| Time and interaction | Speaking time, Q&A, exercises, and any requested slide limit |
| Source material | Verified facts, dates, units, scope, assumptions, and missing evidence |
| Voice and visual context | Language, regional context, brand, template, reference deck, and tone |
| Deliverable | Outline, editable slides, PDF, notes, handout, or another requested format |

For mixed audiences, identify whose decision or learning outcome governs the main story and put specialist detail where it remains accessible. Do not erase material caveats to accommodate a less technical audience.

Read [audience and presentation types](references/audience-and-formats.md) when choosing an argument, density, or delivery approach. Live slides support a speaker; independently read material needs enough context to stand alone. When both are requested, choose a suitable companion or version within the requested scope. Do not force either sparse slides or dense documents onto every assignment.

## Build the argument and storyboard

1. State the central message and audience outcome in plain language. For exploratory work, state the question and what is known instead of manufacturing a recommendation.
2. Select an order suited to the presentation type. A decision deck may lead with a recommendation, while a demonstration may follow a user task. Storytelling can mean a clear explanation or argument; do not add a fictional protagonist or dramatic arc to every deck.
3. Identify the evidence supporting each material claim. Preserve dates, populations, units, denominators, baselines, uncertainty, and the difference between correlation and causation. Distinguish observed results, forecasts, targets, assumptions, and proposals. Do not fill gaps with invented numbers, testimonials, customer stories, or citations.
4. Give each slide a useful purpose. In a storyboard, include the title or main point, essential content, planned evidence or visual, and any speaker context needed. Use the lightest format that makes the sequence reviewable; a full table is optional.
5. Check the sequence for missing reasoning, repeated claims, unexplained jumps, and likely objections. End with the response appropriate to the purpose: a decision, next step, learning recap, or open question. Do not force a sales ask into an informational talk.

Use a supported takeaway title when the slide establishes a finding. Use a direct subject title for an agenda, definition, process, or setup when that serves the audience better. Reading the titles should help reveal the structure, but every title need not be a claim or a slogan.

Keep source references attached to the claims they support during planning. In a deck, put citations in notes or visible source lines as the context requires, keeping material limitations visible to the audience. Independently read exports must retain necessary sources and disclosures even if notes do not travel with the file. Refresh unstable facts when current accuracy matters; do not browse merely to decorate supplied evidence with extra citations.

## Write and design for the actual content

Read [writing and visual review](references/writing-and-visual-review.md) for substantial drafting, rewriting, visual direction, or quality review.

- Prefer concrete actors, actions, objects, comparisons, and consequences. Remove filler that could fit almost any organization. Explain necessary jargon for the audience rather than deleting useful domain language.
- Match certainty to evidence. Simplifying language must not turn a hypothesis into a fact, a pilot into a rollout, or a target into an achieved result.
- Choose visuals that explain the content: a chart for a measured comparison, an editable diagram for a mechanism, a screenshot for actual product behavior, or a photograph for relevant context. Label mockups and synthetic examples where they could be mistaken for evidence.
- Maintain a coherent visual system with purposeful variation. Repeated card grids, decorative icons, gradients, and stock imagery are review signals when they obscure meaning or make the deck interchangeable; they are not universally forbidden styles.
- Preserve requested editability. Do not flatten charts, tables, or diagrams that must remain editable into generated pictures. Keep logos and source artwork faithful. Check relevant usage rights and attribution for external assets.
- Follow audience, brand, and accessibility needs before aesthetic fashion. Protect contrast, readable type, reading order, useful alt text, and captions where appropriate. Do not encode essential distinctions with color or animation alone.

Read [research and 2026 trends](references/research-and-trends.md) when the user requests current trends, a research basis, or a contemporary visual direction. Treat vendor forecasts as optional art direction, not universal effectiveness claims. Refresh dated claims when reused for current recommendations. Keep changing trend names out of the permanent workflow.

## Review and prepare delivery

For substantial work, inspect the argument and the actual deliverable, not just the generated outline or a tool's success message:

- **Audience and purpose:** Does the content answer their question at a useful level? Is the desired response clear where needed?
- **Evidence:** Do claims, chart scales, totals, labels, sources, and material caveats agree? Can a reader distinguish results from proposals and assumptions?
- **Writing:** Is the wording natural, specific, and consistent with the requested voice? Can the presenter explain it? Remove repetitive slogans and unsupported certainty.
- **Visual execution:** Inspect rendered slides for hierarchy, legibility, clipping, overflow, image quality, and brand/template fit. Fix excess content before shrinking it into unreadable text. Check relevant accessibility and requested editability.
- **Delivery:** Where a live presentation is in scope, support it with useful talking points, an achievable timing plan, likely questions, and a fallback for essential demos or media. Do not put a transcript on every slide or generate an unrequested coaching package.

A limited critique need only cover the requested surface. For material or consequential decks, use a fresh review when available and worthwhile; the team lead owns acceptance. Lack of a separate reviewer does not block ordinary work, but do not claim independent review that did not happen.

Distinguish source inspection, rendered-slide inspection, editability checks, application testing, rehearsal, and actual audience acceptance. Automated checks and model agreement cannot establish that people understand or find a presentation persuasive.

## Collaboration and completion

Business analysis supplies accepted objectives, requirements, rules, and evidence. Presentation strategy translates that material into a suitable argument and delivery format. Visual design and authoring implement it. The lead coordinates work and accepts the result; these are responsibilities that one person or agent may combine.

Keep model routing in the team workflow and project facts in their canonical sources. Do not load other companions unless the task needs them. This skill does not authorize publishing, sharing, emailing, or changing access to the deliverable.

Deliver the requested presentation or planning artifact using the authoring workflow's output conventions. Mention limitations that affect use, such as missing evidence, untested media, unavailable rendering, or a file that was not produced. Keep internal validation commentary out of audience-facing slides and notes unless requested.
